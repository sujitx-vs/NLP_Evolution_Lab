### BOW 

While implementing Bag of Words manually,
I preserved tokens such as **"15.99"**, **"c-dac"**,
and **"can't"** by using custom preprocessing rules.
However, when I used Scikit-learn's `CountVectorizer`,
I observed that the generated vocabulary was different. 
By default, `CountVectorizer` applies its own tokenizer, 
which splits tokens based on its internal regular expression. 
As a result, `"15.99"` became `"15"` and `"99"`, `"c-dac"` became `"dac"` (discarding the single-character `"c"`), 
and `"can't"` was split, causing `"n't"` to be removed. 
This experiment showed that the choice of tokenizer and preprocessing strategy directly affects the final vocabulary and the resulting Bag of Words matrix. 
For complete control over tokenization, custom preprocessing should be performed before vectorization.



In TF-IDF:

Common words like AI, is, the are like the school uniform—they appear everywhere,
so they carry little distinguishing information.
Rare words like healthcare, future, or changing are like the bright red cap—they help identify what makes a document unique.

TF= Total words in the document/ Number of times a word appears

The Smart CCTV Search document was converted into numerical vectors using both a manual TF-IDF implementation and Scikit-Learn's TfidfVectorizer. 
The document contained 67 sentences and approximately 270 unique vocabulary terms. 
Each sentence was represented as a sparse TF-IDF vector, where most values were zero because only the words present in a sentence received non-zero weights. 
The Scikit-Learn implementation produced similar feature representations but different numerical values due to its default L2 normalization.
